The psychology of price numbers: charm pricing, round numbers and anchors

Why $9.99 works, when round prices win, and how anchors and decoys shape what customers think is fair.

By the 17381 Numbers Lab editorial team · Updated 2026-11-15 · How we write

Customers do not process prices like calculators. A handful of well-studied effects shape how a number feels — and you can use them honestly to present value clearly.

Charm pricing (the left-digit effect)

People read numbers left to right and anchor on the first digit, so $19.99 often feels closer to $19 than to $20. Charm prices tend to suit value-focused and impulse purchases.

Round numbers signal quality

For premium, emotional or considered purchases, round prices ($200, not $199.99) can feel more confident and are easier to process. Luxury brands rarely use charm endings.

Anchoring

The first number a buyer sees becomes the reference point. Showing a premium tier first makes the middle tier feel reasonable. Anchors must be genuine options — fake "was" prices can break consumer-protection laws in many countries.

The decoy effect

Adding a third option that is clearly worse than one of the others nudges people toward the target option. Three-tier pricing tables work partly for this reason.

Precise numbers feel researched

A quote of $4,870 can feel more carefully calculated than $5,000, which may reduce haggling in negotiations.

Cultural number preferences

In parts of East Asia the number 8 is associated with prosperity and 4 with bad luck, which can influence prices, phone numbers and addresses. See our guide to lucky and unlucky numbers in global commerce.
