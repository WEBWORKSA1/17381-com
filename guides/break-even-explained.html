Break-even analysis explained (with three real-world decisions)

A plain-English guide to break-even: contribution margin, fixed vs variable costs and how to use break-even to decide on prices, hires and rent.

By the 17381 Numbers Lab editorial team · Updated 2026-11-15 · How we write

Break-even analysis answers one question: how much do we need to sell before we stop losing money? It sounds basic, but used properly it is one of the fastest decision tools a business has.

Fixed vs variable costs

Fixed costs stay the same whether you sell one unit or a thousand in a month: rent, salaries, insurance, software. Variable costs rise with each sale: materials, packaging, shipping, payment fees, commissions. Some costs are mixed — split them as honestly as you can.

Contribution margin is the engine

Every sale contributes price minus variable cost towards fixed costs. Sell enough contributions to cover fixed costs and you are at break-even; everything after that is profit.

Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ (Price − Variable cost)

Decision 1: should we raise prices?

A café sells coffee at $4.50 with $1.30 of variable cost and has $9,600 of monthly fixed costs. Break-even is 3,000 cups. At $4.90 contribution rises to $3.60 and break-even falls to 2,667 cups. Even if 8% of customers leave, the café is better off.

Decision 2: can we afford a hire?

Add $3,800 of monthly cost for a barista and break-even rises by 3,800 ÷ 3.20 = about 1,188 cups. Will longer opening hours or faster service generate that? If not, the hire is a bet, not a plan.

Decision 3: bigger premises?

A move that adds $2,000 of rent must add 625 cups a month at the original price just to stand still. Write that number down before signing a lease.

Limitations

  • Assumes a constant price and cost per unit — volume discounts change both.
  • Ignores timing of cash; pair it with a runway forecast.
  • Multi-product businesses need a weighted-average contribution margin.
