Why prime factors protect your credit card (and what 17381 can teach you)

A gentle introduction to prime factorisation and why multiplying primes is easy but splitting them apart is hard — the idea behind RSA.

By the 17381 Numbers Lab editorial team · Updated 2026-11-15 · How we write

Every whole number greater than 1 is either prime or can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way. That idea — the fundamental theorem of arithmetic — quietly protects much of the internet.

A small example: 17381

17381 = 7 × 13 × 191. Multiplying those three primes takes seconds by hand. Going the other way — starting from 17381 and finding the primes — takes more work: you test divisors one by one. Our Number Analyzer does it instantly for numbers this size, because a computer can try every divisor up to √17381 ≈ 132 in microseconds.

Scale changes everything

Now imagine two primes each hundreds of digits long. Multiplying them is still fast. Factoring their product with known classical algorithms would take an impractical amount of time. That one-way asymmetry is the heart of RSA encryption, used for decades to secure websites, email and payments.

Public key, private key

In RSA, the big product is part of the public key — anyone can use it to encrypt a message. Only someone who knows the original prime factors can compute the private key needed to decrypt. Break the factorisation and you break the lock.

What about quantum computers?

Shor's algorithm shows that a sufficiently large, error-corrected quantum computer could factor such numbers efficiently. That is why standards bodies have been publishing and rolling out post-quantum cryptography designed to resist quantum attacks.
